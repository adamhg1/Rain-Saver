RainSaver

A Sensor-Controlled Plant Watering System Powered by Rainwater

A plant watering system that uses rainwater instead of tap water, and only waters when the soil is actually dry.

01

Why it matters

In Metro Vancouver, daily water use rises by about 50 % in the hot, dry summer months, from about 1 billion to more than 1.5 billion litres a day, mostly because of lawn and garden watering (Metro Vancouver, n.d.). Almost all of it is treated drinking water.

British Columbians also use more water at home than the Canadian average of 223 litres per person per day (Statistics Canada, 2023). During the 2023 drought, Metro Vancouver moved to Stage 2 restrictions and banned all lawn watering (City of Vancouver, 2023).

Two simple changes can help. Rainwater collected in a barrel reduces demand on treated drinking water, and it is chlorine-free and at outdoor temperature, which is better for plants (City of Vancouver, n.d.). Soil moisture sensors can prevent watering when the soil is already wet, although research has shown that their accuracy varies and that they save the most water where people over-water (Cardenas-Lailhacar & Dukes, 2010; Dukes, 2012). Low-cost sensors need to be calibrated for the soil they are used in (Placidi et al., 2020). RainSaver combines both ideas.

Question

Can a sensor-controlled system keep a plant's soil moisture between 35 % and 60 % using only collected rainwater?

Hypothesis

If the plant is watered only when the soil drops below 35 %, then the soil will stay in the target range while using less water than watering on a fixed schedule.

02

How it works

Water flows from a raised rain bucket to the plant by gravity, so no pump is needed. An Arduino reads the soil moisture and opens an electric valve only when the soil is too dry.

Watering

The sensor is read every 5 seconds. Below 35 %, the valve opens for up to 30 seconds, then waits 60 seconds for the water to soak in. It stops at 60 %.

Data logging

Every 15 minutes, the Arduino saves the average soil moisture in its own memory. It stores about 10 days of data, and keeps it when unplugged.

Safety

If watering doesn't raise the moisture, the bucket is probably empty, so the system stops. The valve also closes automatically if the power goes out.

Materials

Arduino Uno, soil moisture sensor, 5 V relay, 12 V solenoid valve, 12 V power supply, rain bucket, tubing.

04

Log analyzer

Type d in the Arduino Serial Monitor, copy the output, and paste it below. The analyzer converts the records to real dates and times, graphs them, and calculates summary statistics. Nothing is uploaded; everything runs in your browser.

2. Enter when each power-on started

05

Method

  1. Calibrate the sensor in dry soil and in water, using the same soil as the experiment.
  2. Test the valve with the bucket full, and measure how many mL per second it lets through.
  3. Start the log and write the date and time in the logbook.
  4. Run one automatic pot and one pot watered on a fixed schedule for at least 7 days.
  5. Analyze the saved data with the log analyzer and compare the water used by each pot.
