RainSaver

A Sensor-Controlled Plant Watering System Powered by Rainwater

A plant watering system that uses rainwater instead of tap water, and only waters when the soil is actually dry.

01

Why it matters

In Metro Vancouver, daily water use rises by about 50 % in the hot, dry summer months, from about 1 billion to more than 1.5 billion litres a day, mostly because of lawn and garden watering (Metro Vancouver, n.d.).

British Columbians also use more water at home than the Canadian average of 223 litres per person per day (Statistics Canada, 2023). During the 2023 drought, Metro Vancouver moved to Stage 2 restrictions and banned all lawn watering (City of Vancouver, 2023).

Two simple changes can help. Rainwater collected in a barrel reduces demand on treated drinking water, and it is chlorine-free and at outdoor temperature, which is better for plants (City of Vancouver, n.d.). Soil moisture sensors can prevent watering when the soil is already wet, although research has shown that their accuracy varies and that they save the most water where people over-water (Cardenas-Lailhacar & Dukes, 2010; Dukes, 2012). Low-cost sensors need to be calibrated for the soil they are used in (Placidi et al., 2020). RainSaver combines both ideas.

Question

Will a sensor-controlled irrigation system powered by collected rainwater open the valve on dry soil and stay closed on damp and wet soil, as a model for cutting outdoor water waste in dry months?

Hypothesis

If the soil moisture is below 35 %, then the valve will open and close again at 60 %. If the soil is damp (35 % to 60 %) or wet (above 60 %), then the valve will stay closed, because the system only waters when the sensor reads dry soil.

02

How it works

Water flows from a raised rain bucket to the plant by gravity, so no pump is needed. An Arduino reads the soil moisture and opens an electric valve only when the soil is too dry.

Watering

The sensor is read every 5 seconds. Below 35 %, the valve opens for up to 30 seconds, then waits 60 seconds for the water to soak in. It stops at 60 %.

Data logging

Every 15 minutes, the Arduino saves the average soil moisture in its own memory. It stores about 10 days of data, and keeps it when unplugged.

Safety

If watering does not raise the moisture, the bucket is probably empty, so the system stops. The valve also closes automatically if the power goes out.

Materials

Arduino Uno R3, capacitive soil moisture sensor V2.0.0, 5 V one-channel relay module, 12 V normally closed solenoid valve, 12 V 2 A power adapter, 1N4007 diode, 4-gallon rain bucket, 1/2 inch clear vinyl tubing, brass hose barbs, porcelain pot with soil, plywood base, and a stool to raise the bucket.

05

Method

  1. Build the plumbing. A brass hose barb was fitted through the bottom of the bucket with a gasket, a lock nut and sealant. Teflon tape was wrapped on the threads of two barbs, and one barb was screwed into each side of the valve. Tubing was clamped from the bucket to the valve inlet and from the valve outlet to the pot. The bucket was raised on a stool above the valve and pot.
  2. Leak test. The bucket was filled with water and left on cardboard for 30 minutes. No leaks were seen.
  3. Wire the electronics. The sensor was connected to pin 8 (power), GND and A0 (signal). The relay control side was connected to 5 V, GND and pin 7. The 12 V adapter powered the valve through the relay switch side, and a 1N4007 diode was placed across the valve wires to protect the relay from the voltage spike when the coil switches off.
  4. Calibrate the sensor. The sensor read 562 in open air (dry) and 245 in water (wet). These values were entered in the code. The adapter voltage was 11.7 V with the valve open, above the 11 V minimum.
  5. Run the trials. Dry (below 35 %), damp (35 % to 60 %) and wet (above 60 %) soil were each tested three times for 400 seconds. The moisture and the valve state were recorded every 50 seconds.
  6. Analyze. Moisture was graphed against time for each trial, and the valve behaviour was compared with the hypothesis.

Observations

  • The water looked mostly saturated after the dry soil trials.
  • When a dry soil trial started, the solenoid valve made a click as it opened and the water began to flow through the tubing.
  • The relay module made a click at the same moment, and its lime green light turned on whenever the relay switched on the valve.
  • The sensor readings were a little inconsistent unless the sensor was pushed fully down into the soil.
07

Discussion

The valve opened in all three dry soil trials. The moisture started at 24%, 31% and 21% and rose to 78%, 76% and 71% by 400 seconds. In the three damp soil trials, the moisture started at 40%, 42% and 48% and ended at 41%, 43% and 46%, and in the three wet soil trials it started at 98%, 96% and 94% and ended at 100%, 96% and 93%. The valve stayed closed in all six of these trials.

The data support the hypothesis. In the dry soil trials, the watering stopped at around 60% moisture, as designed. However, as the water settled through the soil, the moisture reading kept rising and ended well above 70% (71% to 78%). The final moisture was therefore higher than the 60% stop point, because the water continued to spread to the sensor after the valve had closed. On dry soil, the moisture rose by 34 to 53 percentage points in the first 100 seconds and then levelled off after about 150 seconds. On damp and wet soil, the readings stayed almost flat. Wet trial 1 stayed at 100%, which is the top of the sensor's scale.

Sources of error

  • Sensor accuracy: the reading changes with how tightly the soil is packed around the sensor.
  • Calibration: the dry and wet readings are only as accurate as the time they were taken.
  • Sensor position: soil moisture is not the same everywhere in a pot.
  • Soil set-up: the damp and wet soil were prepared by hand, so the starting moisture may not be exactly as planned.
  • Short trials: each trial lasted only 400 seconds because the laptop had to stay plugged in.
  • Power supply: the 12 V adapter is close to the valve's needs (about 1.7 A).
  • Few trials: each condition was tested only three times.

Next steps

Tune the stop point, add a water level sensor to the bucket, test more than one plant and soil type, use the data logger to run the system for several days, and test outdoors for several weeks in dry weather. A real garden is much larger and the weather changes, so a larger system would need more sensors and a bigger rain barrel.

08

Conclusion

The system opened the valve in all three dry soil trials (21% to 31% moisture) and kept it closed in all three damp trials (40% to 48%) and all three wet trials (94% to 98%). This supports the hypothesis, except that the watering stopped at around 60% and the moisture then rose to 71% to 78% as the water settled.

These results suggest that a sensor-controlled system powered by rainwater could reduce water waste by watering only when the soil is dry. Future work should tune the stop point, add a water level sensor to the bucket, test more than one plant and soil type, and run the system outdoors for a longer period.
